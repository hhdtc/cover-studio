1961 → 1981
  1. 1961

    First Crewed Suborbital Flight

    A fifteen-minute ballistic arc. Congress appropriates the rest of the decade.

  2. 1965

    First Orbital Rendezvous

    Two spacecraft hold station-keeping at 36 meters. On purpose, with paperwork.

    Crewed Lunar Landing

    The second crew arrives to find the station cold but salvageable. They stay 59 days.

    The truck to low Earth orbit opens for business. Commuters wanted.

Chapter Seven

The Long Range Era

Before the jump lanes were charted, range was a budget line. A hauler that could cross the Narion line without convoy was a rumor with a fuel bill, and the registries treated every such rumor as a clerical error waiting to be filed.

The refit yards changed the arithmetic. Grav-drive retrofits, sold at first as weather equipment, let ordinary hulls make in a week what had been a season's voyage. Insurance followed, then timetables, then passengers with opinions.

Deepquay grew from a fuel depot into a customs town almost overnight. Its ledgers from the period show the strain: berths oversold three deep, emblems registered and re-registered, and a harbormaster's office that kept two bottles for exactly one drawer of forms.

Historians argue about the era's borders. The people who lived it simply remember the schedules: departures at odd hours, arrivals unannounced, and the long quiet of a lane nobody had thought to name yet.

        Chapter Nine

        Berth Fees & Other Weather

        Berth fees at Deepquay doubled the same season the comet returned, and nobody at the harbormaster's office pretended the two events were unrelated. A port learns what its traffic will bear the way a hull learns which seams weep: slowly, then all at once, then in an invoice.

        The old pilots paid without argument. They had seen charts sold by the inch and fuel sold by rumor, and considered a doubling merely weather. It was the newcomers who wrote letters — four pages, fountain pen, carbon copy — to a board that filed every one under W for weather.

        By the third return of the comet, the fee schedule ran to eleven pages and included a surcharge for vessels deemed romantically rigged. The survey corps asked, in writing, whether romance could be measured. The board replied that it could be taxed, which was nearly the same thing.

        What the ledgers remember is this: the fees rose, the traffic rose faster, and the queue at the customs window kept its good humor through all of it. Optimism, like berth fees, was understood to be part of the cost of doing business among the settled systems.
